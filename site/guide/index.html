First-use guide

How to test one decision with Jev!Jev

Jev is TypeSafe's model for typed decisions; this tool calls it as jev-1.13.0. Jev!Jev tests one decision from your workflow on your own cases; it is not a cross-task benchmark of Jev.

Jev!Jev compares three ways of making one workflow decision on the same test cases: what you do now (an LLM), a simple rule, and Jev. Jev picks one answer from a fixed list for each text; it does not write text. You judge every answer, the tool adds up what each one cost, and a fixed rule gives one of three verdicts.

Read sections 1 to 4 once, then walk the sample run with section 5 open beside it. Every field you will see is in the field glossary at the end. Words in double quotes are labels exactly as the product shows them on screen. On the command line instead of the browser? The command-line guide runs the same steps with bun run decide.

The sample run page and this guide are open to everyone. The workspace is the signed-in page where you run your own test; its title on screen is "Backstage Pass". The workspace and its sample cases file need you to sign in. To get access, use "Go backstage with my own decision" in the section headed "Ticket office" (open it) at the foot of the sample run page. A person reads each request and replies by email; if your decision suits a test, the reply explains how to sign in (email and password, or Continue with Google for an invited address). The request alone does not open the workspace. You also need your own TypeSafe API key: get it from the TypeSafe Playground.

1. Prepare test cases

A test case is one piece of text your workflow has to decide about, such as one customer message.

  • Get a TypeSafe API key. The workspace calls Jev with your own key, from the TypeSafe Playground. Calls are billed to your TypeSafe account at $0.042 per million input tokens, output free.
  • Pick one decision with a fixed answer set. In the workspace that is two answers. Write the question in One question, name the answers in First answer and Second answer, and say what each one means before you see any model output.
  • Write the cases. Paste them into Cases, one per line, or use Import cases CSV with the columns case_id,case_input (sample cases file, signed in). The workspace takes 1 to 100 cases of up to 8,000 characters each.
  • Plan for at least 30 cases you will label. A verdict needs 30 paired, labelled cases (section 4). Mix easy, hard and ambiguous cases.
  • Synthetic or redacted text only. Each case is sent to every provider you select. No real names, emails, account or order numbers.
  • Or start from a ready pack. In the step labelled "New Scene", packs P1 to P4 come with 10 labelled cases and P5 to P8 with 30 cases for you to label.

A case file can carry labels too: add a label column (one of your two answers), then label_source, labelled_by and labelled_at. A label with no label_source is stored as agent and is never counted; one marked human is stored as human_reviewed.

2. Label acceptance

Labelling is you deciding, case by case, what the right answer is. An answer that matches your label is kept (the file says accept); one that does not is rejected (reject). Kept and rejected answers both count: each labelled answer is in its player's accept rate (kept out of labelled) and in the paired comparison the verdict uses, and every answer's cost is in its player's spend. Cost per kept answer divides that whole spend by the kept answers only, so rejected answers make each kept one dearer: one kept and one rejected answer at $1 each cost $2 per kept answer.

  1. Write the rule first. In the step labelled "New Scene", fill Keep an answer when... with the test you will apply to every answer, and Leave out... with cases this test does not cover. For the sample shop bot the rule was: answer when the fact sheet covers everything asked; hand_off when it needs live stock, a booking, a policy not on the sheet, a safety judgement or a complaint.
  2. Calibrate. The step labelled "Rehearsals" starts with 5 practice cases whose reference labels a person approved. Your agreement shows at the end.
  3. Settle retries, then open judging. Once the run has stopped, retry any unfinished calls in the step labelled "Learning Lines" first (Retry unfinished calls). Then click Open blind judging and lock retries (Open judging and lock retries in a Jev-only run). From that click on, retries are refused and the set of answers is fixed.
  4. Pick blind. For each case, choose one of your two named answers (the buttons carry the names you gave as First answer and Second answer) or Unsure, before any answer is shown. That first pick is the label every player is scored against.
  5. Then keep or change. After your pick, Jev's options for the case appear ranked by Jev's probability for each; if Jev returned no probabilities, its choice comes first and the rest are marked unranked. If the run has no Jev answer for the case, you see "No suggestion yet" and can only keep your blind pick; with a TypeSafe key entered, Ask Jev with your key (one paid call) makes one Jev call billed to your TypeSafe account at Jev's list price ($0.042 per million input tokens, output free), and its cost counts in Total actual attempts. A change is recorded beside your blind pick, never instead of it.
  6. Unsure means unlabelled. An unsure case is never guessed. Its answers are left out of the accept rates and the verdict's pairing (shown as unlabelled and excluded), but what they cost still counts in spend and so in the cost per kept answer the tables show.
  7. Imported labels. A case you do not pick keeps its imported human_reviewed label and is scored on it. Any pick you make on the case, Unsure included, replaces the imported label for that case.
  8. Finish judging and lock labels. This reveals the players and unlocks results and downloads. Labels are locked after it.

Where a label came from is recorded with it: human (a person picked it in the workspace), human_reviewed (a person approved it but did not pick it there, such as an approved AI draft) or agent (an AI made it and nobody reviewed it). agent labels are never counted as truth. The label card is the one-page version.

3. Enter costs

In the workspace, costs are entered for you: each answer's cost is worked out from the token usage the provider returns and a dated list price. You enter costs by hand only in your own results file (below). In the workspace you see three things:

  • Before the run: the step labelled "Learning Lines" shows an estimate, such as "up to N paid calls", from dated list prices, about 1 to 4 bytes of your text per input token and each model's output cap. Your invoice may differ.
  • After the run: the step labelled "Dress Rehearsal" shows Answers-only spend and Cost / kept per player, and Total actual attempts, which also counts failed and retried calls.
  • A simple rule costs $0. It runs locally and makes no API call.

jnj-record/1 is a format name, not a file name: jnj-record is the results-record format and the number after the slash is its version, so jnj-record/1.1 and jnj-record/1.2 are later minor versions of the same format.

Bringing your own results file (Load your own script (CSV) on the sample run page, format jnj-record/1) means you enter the cost yourself, one cost_usd cell per row:

  • The cost of that one call in US dollars, as a plain number (0.00003242, not "about a cent"). Use 0 for a rule.
  • Leave it empty if you do not know it. The file stays valid, but that player's spend shows as incomplete, and a missing cost on a paired Jev or LLM row makes the verdict "not enough evidence".
  • Optionally add price_table_date (YYYY-MM-DD), the date of the price list you used.

Every column a results file can carry, in header order. The columns up to latency_ms are required in every file. The rest may be left out of the header, with conditions: a labelled row in a jnj-record/1.1 or jnj-record/1.2 file must fill labelled_by, labelled_at and label_blind, and a jnj-record/1 file leaves them empty; label_final and suggestion_shown need jnj-record/1.1 or jnj-record/1.2; outcome and the decisions answerer need jnj-record/1.2. The full rules are in the format reference.

4. Read the verdict

The verdict compares Jev with the LLM on paired cases: cases where both have a labelled answer. Cases missing either label are left out. It also compares Jev with the rule on accept rate only, because a rule costs nothing. The rules below run in order and the first that matches wins. The screen says which rule fired, by the number below, and why.

Two intervals feed it. The accept-rate difference (Jev minus the LLM, or the rule minus Jev) gets a confidence interval by Newcombe's paired method; its lower bound is the most pessimistic value the evidence allows. The cost ratio (Jev's cost per kept answer divided by the LLM's) gets an interval from 2,000 resamples of the paired cases.

Newcombe's paired method: a standard way to put a range around the difference between two kept shares measured on the same cases. It starts from a separate range for each share, then allows for both answerers having seen the same messages, which is what paired means. The upper bound is the largest value in the range and the lower bound the smallest. A higher value favours Jev for Jev minus the LLM and favours the rule for the rule minus Jev; a higher cost ratio means Jev is dearer, so there a lower value favours Jev. Each verdict is uncorrected: it compares Jev with one other answerer on its own, and nothing widens the ranges when several comparisons run at once, so with several LLMs one of them can cross a bound by chance.

  1. Rule 1: Not enough evidence when there are no Jev answers or no LLM answers; when Jev and the LLM have fewer than 30 paired labelled cases (the screen says how many more to add); when neither kept any answer; or when a paired Jev or LLM row has no cost.
  2. Rule 2: Don't use Jev when any of these holds:
    • the rule is within 10 points of Jev: the lower bound of the rule minus Jev is above -0.10, on at least 30 paired rule cases. A free rule does the job.
    • Jev is clearly worse than the LLM: the upper bound of Jev minus the LLM is below -0.10.
    • Jev is clearly dearer: the lower bound of the cost ratio is above one.
  3. Rule 3: Use Jev when all of these hold: the lower bound of Jev minus the LLM is above -0.10 (Jev is within 10 points of the LLM); the cost ratio is 0.8 or less (at least 20% cheaper per kept answer); and the cost ratio's upper bound is below one.
  4. Rule 4: Not enough evidence otherwise. The screen names every condition of the use-Jev rule that was not met. It gives no count of extra cases here.

"Not enough evidence" is a finding, not a failure: the cases did not show a difference either way within the margin. If only one of Jev and the LLM kept nothing, the order above matters: the two accept-rate conditions of "don't use Jev" are checked first, so Jev keeping nothing while the LLM kept nearly everything is "don't use Jev" (Jev clearly worse). Only when neither of those fires is a one-sided zero "not enough evidence", with the labels to check named. Two cost guards sit at the same point, after those two accept-rate conditions and before "Jev is clearly dearer": when Jev and the LLM both cost $0 per kept answer there is no cost ratio, and when the costs are too large to add up or divide the ratio is not finite. Either gives "not enough evidence", reported as rule one. The same rules run in the workspace (the step labelled "Opening Night"), on the sample run page when you load your own file, and in bun run decide verdict <file> --question <id>.

5. Worked example: the sample run

Open the sample run page and scroll. Everything on it is a recorded run, flagged Sample run in the corner: 40 made-up messages to a ski shop's bot, and one decision, "can the bot answer this from its fact sheet, or must it hand off to staff?"

Captions that are not results

Some captions on the sample run page are decoration or navigation, not results:

  • "ADMIT ONE", "ROW J", "SEAT J-EV" are printed on the entrance card shown before the run. Decoration only: the J is for Jev, and there is nothing to book.
  • The button labelled "SKIP THE OVERTURE" shows the run at once instead of as you scroll. The link labelled "GO TO THE TICKET OFFICE" jumps to the section headed "Ticket office" at the foot of the page, where you ask to watch a recorded test or for access to the workspace.
  • "Should I use Jev" with its "Ask" button: typing your own question is switched off until the live check ships. The example buttons under it show prepared answers from the sample data; no Jev call is made.
  • The line "Understudies wait in the wings; the stage manager keeps tabs on the subs." in the second part of the run is decoration. It says nothing about the run.
  • The list headed "IN REPERTOIRE" at the end names UC1 to UC10: other decisions Jev could be tested on, each with its question. They are not run on this page.

The question and the three players

A, What you do now is an LLM answering each message from the fact sheet. B, A simple rule is a keyword list that sends stock, booking, policy and safety words to staff. C, Jev decides picks answer or hand off, with a confidence. The second answer's name is hand_off, as the workspace and the results file write it; the sample run page prints it as "hand off". The coloured bar under each player shows how often it picked each answer.

The labels

Every message has one label, the right answer: 20 answer and 20 hand off. Three AI labellers who never saw any player's answer drafted them from the fact sheet, and a person reviewed and approved them, so they are human_reviewed, not blind picks. The acceptance rule is the one quoted under Label acceptance.

The cases table

Each row is one message. Case is its id, Message its text, and the small label tag after the text is the right answer. Under A now, B rule and C Jev each player's pick is marked kept if it matches the label, rejected if not. Jev said repeats Jev's pick with its confidence and a bar.

Jev's mistakes come first, highest confidence first. The row marked "Spotlight" is m33, "If it dumps overnight can I swap my demo skis for something wider?" The fact sheet promises free condition swaps on demo rentals, so the label is answer. Jev said hand off with 0.78 confidence, so its answer was rejected. A high confidence on a rejected answer is why confidence is the model's own score, not measured accuracy. Kept so far counts kept answers as rows scroll into view.

The verdict

The verdict is Not enough evidence, and the line under it reads Rule 4 fired. Walking the rules in order:

  1. Rule one passes: Jev and the LLM have 40 paired labelled cases, at least 30, and every paired row has a cost.
  2. Rule two does not fire. The rule is not within the margin: the lower bound of the rule minus Jev is -0.38, not above -0.10. Jev is not clearly worse: the upper bound of Jev minus the LLM is 0.11, not below -0.10. Jev is not dearer: the cost ratio is 0.013.
  3. Rule three fails on one condition. Cost passes: the ratio 0.013 is under 0.8 and its upper bound 0.014 is below one. Accept rate does not: both kept the same share, but on 40 cases the lower bound of Jev minus the LLM is -0.11, just below the -0.10 the margin needs.
  4. So rule four fires: not enough evidence. The caveat line on the page quotes the reason: "Jev is not shown within 10 points of the LLM (lower bound of Jev minus LLM -0.11, not above -0.10)".

The verdict line in plain words. Jev kept 38 and the LLM 38 of the 40 paired cases, so the best single guess of Jev minus the LLM is no difference. A sample this small still leaves room for luck, so the method puts a range around that guess, from -0.11 to 0.11. "Lower bound of Jev minus LLM -0.11" is the bottom of that range: the worst gap this evidence cannot rule out, with Jev keeping that much smaller a share of answers than the LLM. The use-Jev rule needs the bottom above -0.10, Jev at worst 10 points behind. -0.11 is not above it, so that condition of rule three is not met and rule four fires. It does not say Jev is worse; more paired cases would narrow the range and could settle it either way.

Read it this way: on this test set Jev matched the LLM's kept answers at a small fraction of the cost, but the cases are too few to rule out Jev being more than 10 points worse. The page shows the reason line; the rule-two and cost-ratio figures above come from the same verdict command run on the sample's records file.

6. Limitations

  • Test-set evidence, not a production guarantee. Every verdict carries the line "These results are for this test set only, not production." New kinds of input can behave differently.
  • Synthetic or redacted data only. This is a demo service. The workspace sends each case through our server to every provider you select.
  • One workflow shape. One question with a fixed answer set, one text per case, judged one case at a time. Workspace questions have two answers and its keyword rule picks the first answer when any keyword matches. Jev does not write text, rank or chat.
  • List prices, not invoices. Costs are returned token usage times a dated list price. Your invoice may differ, prices change after the table date, and failed calls can still be charged.
  • Small samples rarely decide. The verdict needs at least 30 paired labelled cases, and at 30 the published simulation finds "not enough evidence" the most common result. See the verdict rules.
  • Labels decide everything. The verdict is only as good as your labels. agent labels never count; the sample run's labels are AI drafts a person approved, not blind human picks.
  • Three players only. The verdict reads the LLM, the rule and Jev. An optional fourth arm in the command line (the OpenAI Decisions API) is recorded but not judged.
  • Several models, several verdicts. When the workspace compares Jev with more than one LLM, each pair gets its own uncorrected verdict and no overall winner is picked.
  • Size limits. The workspace takes 1 to 100 cases of up to 8,000 characters each; the sample run page reads a results file up to 5 MB.
  • Confidence is not accuracy. Jev's confidence is the model's own score; the sample's case marked "Spotlight" was rejected at 0.78.
